# Staff / Principal FDE, Principal Solutions Architect, Field CTO and Head of Forward Deployed Engineering

Senior customer-facing technical leadership: owning the technical side of the most strategic accounts, advising customer executives, setting how the company deploys its product, building and leading FDE or SA teams, and turning field evidence into product direction.

## The role

**Day to day.** The work depends on whether the role is an individual contributor (Staff/Principal FDE, Principal SA, many Field CTOs) or a manager (Head of FDE, Director of Solutions Architecture), but both spend their time on leverage rather than on one deliverable:

- **Strategic accounts:** owning the technical relationship and multi-quarter plan for a handful of the largest or most important customers; joining the hardest escalations; being the person the customer's CTO or CIO calls.
- **Executive advisory:** presenting AI or platform strategy to customer executives and boards, running executive briefings, answering "should we build our own model?" type questions honestly.
- **Methodology:** defining how engagements run end to end (scoping, pilot, production, handover), the reference architectures, the evaluation and security standards, and the reusable assets.
- **Team building:** hiring, levelling, coaching and staffing engineers across accounts; designing the interview loop; deciding which customers get forward deployed help.
- **Product influence:** turning patterns seen across many customers into roadmap changes, and deciding what stays custom versus what becomes product.
- **Commercial partnership:** working with sales and customer success leaders on deal strategy, pilots, pricing of services and renewals, without becoming a salesperson.
- **External voice (mainly Field CTO):** conference talks, analyst briefings, thought-leadership writing, and customer advisory boards.

**Common titles / aliases.** Staff or Principal Forward Deployed Engineer, Principal Solutions Architect, Distinguished or Chief Architect (field), Field CTO, Office of the CTO (field-facing), Head / Director of Forward Deployed Engineering, Director of Solutions Architecture or Solutions Engineering, Head of Applied AI (customer-facing), Engagement Director (deployment-led companies). The same title can mean an individual contributor at one company and a manager of managers at another; read the job description for "manages a team of" or "reports".

**Companies that hire for it (examples).** AI labs and AI-native companies that run forward deployed teams (for example OpenAI, Anthropic and Scale AI have advertised FDE and applied AI leadership roles), Palantir (deployment leadership), data and infrastructure vendors where "Field CTO" is a common title (for example Databricks, Snowflake, MongoDB, Confluent and Elastic have used it), cloud providers (principal and specialist SA leadership at AWS, Microsoft and Google Cloud), and late-stage enterprise SaaS companies building their first FDE or SA function. Titles and team structures change often; check current postings.

**How it differs from a senior FDE.**

| | Staff / Principal FDE, Field CTO, Head of FDE | Senior Forward Deployed Engineer |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Portfolio of strategic accounts, the whole field organisation, or a market segment | One or two customer deployments |
| Main output | Strategy, methodology, reference designs, decisions, a strong team, product direction | Working code and a successful deployment |
| Audience | Customer C-suite and boards, own company's executives, product leadership | Customer engineers and managers, own team |
| Time horizon | Multiple quarters to years | Weeks to a quarter |
| How success is judged | Revenue retained and expanded, deployment success rate across accounts, time to value, team quality, product changes driven | Customer outcome for that deployment, code quality, speed |
| Coding | Still expected to be credible; writes prototypes and reviews designs, rarely owns production code | Daily |

**Who it suits.** Experienced engineers (often ten or more years, with several in customer-facing or tech-lead roles) who enjoy multiplying other people's impact, are comfortable in a room with executives, can still go deep technically when an escalation needs it, and accept that much of their impact is indirect.

| Process and methodology design | High |

**Career paths.** Distinguished Engineer or Chief Architect, VP of Field Engineering or Customer Engineering, CTO or Chief AI Officer at a customer or a startup, VP of Product or Head of Platform (from the product-influence side), founder (many field leaders start companies after seeing repeated customer problems), or partner-level roles at consulting firms.

## Model answers

### Role and scope

#### How does a staff or principal forward deployed engineer differ from a senior FDE?

The difference is scope and leverage, not more hours of coding.

- **A senior FDE** delivers one deployment very well: scopes it, builds it, earns the customer's trust and ships an outcome.
- **A staff FDE** is accountable for outcomes across several deployments or one very large, ambiguous one. They set the technical approach other FDEs follow, unblock the hardest problems, and create reusable patterns (a reference architecture, an eval harness, a connector) that make the next deployment faster.
- **A principal FDE** shapes the organisation and the product: they define methodology, are trusted by customer executives and by the company's own leadership, and change the roadmap based on patterns across many customers.

Signals interviewers look for: you describe your impact through other people ("three teams adopted the pattern"), you choose which problems to work on rather than being assigned them, you write things down (design docs, playbooks) that outlive your involvement, and you can still go deep enough to debug a production incident. A good closing line: "At senior level I measure myself on the deployment. At staff and above I measure myself on how many deployments succeed without me."

#### What does a Field CTO do, and how is it different from a VP of Engineering or a Head of Solutions Architecture?

A **Field CTO** is a senior technical leader who faces outward: the company's most credible technical voice with customers, prospects, analysts and the market. Typical work: executive briefings with strategic accounts, joining large deals and escalations at the C-level, shaping the technical story of the product, speaking at conferences, and bringing structured market insight back to product and engineering. Many Field CTOs are individual contributors with a small team or none.

- **VP of Engineering** faces inward: owns the engineering organisation that builds the product, its delivery, quality and people. Customer contact is occasional.
- **Head of Solutions Architecture / FDE** owns a team and its operation: hiring, staffing, methodology, utilisation and the results of every engagement.

Short version: the VP of Engineering builds the product, the Head of SA or FDE runs the team that deploys it, and the Field CTO represents the technical vision to the market and connects the two. The exact split varies by company, so ask what decisions the role owns and who reports to it.

#### What would your first 90 days look like as Head of Forward Deployed Engineering?

**Days 1–30: listen and learn.**
- One-to-ones with every engineer, and with the heads of sales, customer success, product and engineering.
- Visit or join calls with the top five to ten accounts; read past engagement documents and post-mortems.
- Build a fact base: active engagements, staffing, deployment success rate, time to first value, escalations, what customers say.

**Days 31–60: diagnose and pick a few changes.**
- Share a written diagnosis: what works, the two or three biggest problems (for example no common scoping process, too much custom code, engineers spread across too many accounts).
- Agree the function's charter and metrics with my manager and peers.
- Fix one visible problem quickly to build trust, such as an engagement intake and prioritisation process.

**Days 61–90: set direction.**
- Publish the operating model: engagement types, entry criteria, stages, quality gates, handover.
- A hiring plan and a levelling guide; start the first hires.
- A product feedback process agreed with product leadership.

Throughout, keep at least one strategic account myself so my judgement stays grounded in real customer work.

### Strategic accounts and account strategy

#### Exercise: build a multi-quarter technical account strategy for a strategic customer.

Structure the answer as a short plan document:

1. **Customer context:** their business strategy, top priorities this year, technology landscape, buying centre and budget cycles.
2. **Current state:** what they use today, adoption and value delivered, health signals (usage, sentiment, escalations), relationships we have and lack.
3. **Goals for 12 to 18 months:** for the customer (business outcomes) and for us (retention, expansion, a reference case). Make both measurable.
4. **Use-case roadmap:** prioritised use cases by value and feasibility, sequenced so each one builds data, integrations or trust for the next.
5. **Relationship plan:** executive sponsor on each side, a technical champion, a regular executive business review, coverage gaps to close.
6. **Risks and mitigations:** sponsor leaving, a competitor, security or procurement blockers, budget cuts.
7. **Resourcing:** who from our side works on it, for how long, and what we need from the customer.
8. **Quarterly milestones and review cadence.**

In the interview, ask clarifying questions first (industry, size, current spend, health), state assumptions, and put the customer's outcomes before the vendor's revenue. Close by naming the first three actions you would take next week.

#### How do you decide which customers get forward deployed engineers, and how many?

FDE time is the scarcest resource in the field, so allocate it deliberately with explicit criteria agreed with sales and customer success leadership:

- **Strategic value:** current and potential revenue, logo value, and whether the use case is repeatable for other customers.
- **Probability of success:** a clear problem, an executive sponsor, access to data and systems, a customer team that can own the result.
- **Learning value:** whether the deployment pushes the product into a segment or capability we care about.
- **Cost to serve:** how much custom work is needed and whether it can become product.

Score candidate engagements, review them monthly in a small allocation forum, and publish the decisions so sales knows the rules. Staff deployments with a small core team (often one or two engineers plus a lead) for a fixed phase with exit criteria, rather than open-ended embedding. Keep a small reserve of capacity for escalations. Revisit allocations at each phase gate: if the customer is not providing access or sponsorship, move the engineers to where they will have impact.

#### A strategic account is at risk of churning six months before renewal. What do you do?

1. **Get the facts within days:** usage trends, open escalations, sentiment from the account team, and direct conversations with the customer's sponsor and the people who use the product daily. Find the real reason: lack of value, a failed deployment, a new executive, budget, or a competitor.
2. **Take ownership on our side:** form a small account recovery team (account executive, customer success, a senior engineer) with one leader and a weekly cadence.
3. **Meet the executive sponsor** with a candid assessment: what went wrong, what we will do, and what we need from them. Do not defend past mistakes.
4. **Agree a recovery plan** with two or three measurable outcomes achievable well before the renewal, such as fixing the top reliability issue and getting one high-value use case into production.
5. **Deliver visibly:** short weekly updates, early wins, and executive engagement from our leadership if appropriate.
6. **Prepare the renewal** around demonstrated value with numbers, not discounts alone.
7. **Run a blameless review** afterwards to find why the risk was spotted late, and add an early-warning signal to account health reviews.

#### How do you map and manage stakeholders in a large enterprise account?

Build and maintain a stakeholder map, not just a contact list:

- **Who:** economic buyer (owns budget), executive sponsor, technical decision-makers (CTO, chief architect, CISO), business owners of each use case, champions, end users, procurement and legal, and likely blockers.
- **For each:** what they care about, how they measure success, their view of us (supporter, neutral, sceptic), how much influence they have, and who on our side owns the relationship.

Then manage it:
- Match levels: our executives with theirs, our engineers with theirs, with one person owning overall account coordination so the customer hears one voice.
- Give each stakeholder the evidence they care about: ROI for the CFO, risk controls for the CISO, time saved for users.
- Turn sceptics into informed neutrals by involving them early (for example inviting security to design reviews rather than presenting at the end).
- Watch for change: reorganisations and new executives are the most common hidden cause of churn, so refresh the map every quarter.

#### How do you expand from one use case to many inside an enterprise account?

Expansion comes from delivered value and reusable foundations, not from pitching more.

1. **Make the first use case a clear success** with a measured business result, a happy sponsor and a team at the customer who owns it.
2. **Publicise it internally at the customer:** a short case study, a demo at their internal forums, the sponsor presenting results to peers.
3. **Build reusable foundations:** the data connections, security approvals, identity integration and evaluation process from the first use case should make the second one faster and cheaper. Point this out explicitly.
4. **Run a use-case discovery session** with other business units, prioritising by value and feasibility.
5. **Create a governance forum,** such as a joint AI steering group or centre of excellence, so new use cases have a route to funding and approval.
6. **Enable the customer's own teams** to build the next use cases with our platform, so growth is not limited by our engineers' time.

Measure expansion as number of production use cases, active users across business units and revenue, and report it in executive business reviews.

### Executive advisory and presentations

#### Exercise: present a 20-minute AI strategy recommendation to a customer's executive committee. How do you structure it?

Use an answer-first structure and leave half the time for discussion.

1. **The recommendation in one sentence (1 minute):** "Focus the next 12 months on three AI use cases in customer operations, worth an estimated X, funded in two phases."
2. **Why now (2 minutes):** their business pressures and what peers are doing, briefly.
3. **The three use cases (4 minutes):** value, feasibility, time to first result, and why these over others; show the prioritisation matrix rather than a long list.
4. **What it takes (3 minutes):** data, platform, people, operating model, and the risk controls (security, privacy, quality evaluation, human oversight).
5. **Roadmap and decisions needed (2 minutes):** phases with go/no-go gates, budget, the sponsor and the decision you want today.
6. **Discussion (8 minutes).**

Delivery rules: one message per slide, numbers with stated assumptions, no unexplained jargon, an appendix for technical detail. Anticipate the CFO's ROI question, the CISO's data question and the COO's "what happens to my people" question. In the interview, explicitly say what you would ask before preparing it (audience, decision required, constraints).

#### A customer's CEO asks whether they should train or build their own model. What do you advise?

Start from their goal, then usually recommend against training from scratch unless there is a strong reason.

- **Ask why:** competitive differentiation, data privacy, cost, or a belief that they need to "own the AI".
- **Explain the options in order of cost and effort:** use a strong existing model with good prompting and retrieval over their data (RAG); add tools and workflows; fine-tune an existing model for a specific format, style or task; train a model from scratch.
- **Explain the trade-off:** training from scratch needs large amounts of high-quality data, specialist talent, significant compute budget and ongoing maintenance, and the result often falls behind frontier models within months. Their differentiation usually comes from their proprietary data, workflows and distribution, which they can use without training a foundation model.
- **Address the real concern directly:** privacy and control can be handled with enterprise agreements, data controls, private networking or self-hosted open-weight models.
- **Recommend a path:** start with use cases on existing models, build an evaluation set, and revisit fine-tuning or custom models when a measured gap justifies it.

Be candid that if they are an AI company whose product is the model, the answer can be different.

#### How do you explain AI risks and limitations to a board without killing the project?

Present risk as something managed, with the same structure boards use for any other risk.

- **Be specific, not abstract:** name the three or four risks that apply to this use case (incorrect answers, leakage of sensitive data, bias in decisions, over-reliance by staff, regulatory exposure), not a generic list.
- **For each risk:** likelihood, impact, the controls in place (evaluation sets with pass thresholds, human review for high-impact decisions, access controls, logging and monitoring, red-teaming), and the residual risk.
- **Compare with today:** the current manual process has error rates too; show the baseline so AI is judged fairly.
- **Show a staged rollout:** limited scope, measurement, expansion only when thresholds are met, and a clear way to switch it off.
- **Name accountability:** who owns the model's behaviour, who monitors it, and how incidents are handled.
- **Mention regulation briefly** where relevant (for example the EU AI Act, sector regulators, data protection laws) and how the design complies.

Boards approve risky projects all the time; what they reject is risk that nobody understands or owns.

#### How do you quantify and present the return on investment of an AI deployment to a CFO?

Build a simple, conservative model with assumptions the CFO can challenge.

- **Baseline:** current volume, cost per unit (for example cost per support ticket or per document processed), cycle time and error rate, using the customer's own data where possible.
- **Benefits:** cost reduction (time saved multiplied by loaded cost, only where the time is actually redeployed or hiring avoided), revenue effects (conversion, retention), risk reduction (fewer errors or penalties), and speed.
- **Costs:** licences and usage (model tokens, platform), implementation services, integration, internal staff time, change management, and ongoing operations and evaluation.
- **Metrics:** payback period, net benefit over three years, and a sensitivity table showing low, expected and high cases.
- **Evidence:** pilot results measured against a control group or the baseline, not vendor benchmarks.

Present the conservative case first, and separate hard savings from softer benefits. CFOs trust a smaller number they can verify more than a large number they cannot. Offer to agree the measurement method before the pilot so results are accepted later.

#### The customer's CIO has publicly committed to a deadline your team believes is impossible. How do you handle it?

1. **Check the belief:** break the plan down with the team and identify exactly what cannot fit and why (dependencies, data access, security review, scope).
2. **Talk to the CIO privately and early,** never in a large meeting first. Acknowledge why the date matters to them; their credibility is at stake.
3. **Offer options rather than a "no":**
   - Keep the date with reduced scope: what can credibly be live by then (for example one business unit, one workflow, human-in-the-loop mode).
   - Keep the scope and move the date, with a plan showing the realistic one.
   - Add resources or remove blockers that only they can remove (data access, faster security approval, dedicated customer staff), with honest limits on what that achieves.
4. **Help them communicate it:** frame the reduced-scope launch as phase one of a plan, with dates for later phases.
5. **Write down the agreement** and track it visibly.

The worst outcome is to stay quiet and miss the date; a senior field leader protects the customer's executive from a public failure.

### Deployment methodology

#### Exercise: design a repeatable deployment methodology for forward deployed engagements.

Define stages, each with entry criteria, outputs and a go/no-go gate.

1. **Qualify:** confirm the problem, executive sponsor, success metric, data access and budget. Output: a one-page engagement brief. Gate: allocation forum approves.
2. **Discover (1–3 weeks):** workflow mapping with users, data and systems assessment, security and compliance requirements, baseline measurement. Output: scope, success criteria agreed with the customer in writing, an evaluation set.
3. **Prototype / pilot (4–8 weeks):** build on the product, using reference architectures; measure against the baseline with real users. Gate: success criteria met, security approved.
4. **Production:** hardening, monitoring, runbooks, access controls, rollout plan, training. Gate: production readiness review.
5. **Handover and scale:** the customer team or our support organisation owns operations; documentation and enablement; plan the next use case.
6. **Retrospective:** what worked, what product gaps appeared, reusable assets to harvest.

Supporting pieces: templates for each output, reference architectures, a shared library of connectors and evaluation tooling, standard status reporting, and engagement health metrics. Keep it light enough that engineers use it: a methodology is a set of defaults and checklists, not a bureaucracy, and it should change after every retrospective.

#### How do you decide what goes into the core product and what stays as custom customer work?

Use explicit criteria and a regular forum with product leadership:

- **Frequency:** has the same need appeared at three or more customers, or will it clearly appear in the target segment?
- **Fit with strategy:** does it strengthen the product's direction, or pull it towards one customer's niche?
- **Generality:** can it be built as a configurable capability rather than one customer's logic?
- **Cost of keeping it custom:** maintenance burden across forks, support load, upgrade breakage.
- **Revenue impact:** deals won, retained or blocked.

In practice: build custom first, inside a clear extension point (plugin, configuration, separate service) so it does not fork the core. Tag custom work in a shared catalogue. When a pattern repeats, the field writes a short proposal with evidence (customers, revenue, workaround cost) and either product adopts it or the field team turns it into a supported reusable module. Avoid both extremes: a product that is just a pile of customer specials, and a field team that rebuilds the same thing for every customer because product will not take it.

#### How do you make evaluation and quality gates a standard part of every AI deployment?

Make evaluation part of the methodology, the tooling and the definition of done, not an optional extra.

- **Discovery output includes an evaluation set:** representative inputs with expected outputs or grading rubrics, built with the customer's domain experts, including hard and adversarial cases.
- **Success criteria are written as eval thresholds** (for example accuracy on the set, groundedness, refusal behaviour, latency, cost per task) and agreed with the customer before building.
- **Shared tooling:** a standard evaluation harness, LLM-as-judge templates calibrated against human labels, and dashboards, so every team does not build its own.
- **Gates:** no move to pilot or production without a recorded eval run that meets thresholds; every prompt, model or retrieval change runs the eval suite in CI.
- **Production monitoring:** sampling of live traffic for human review, user feedback signals, drift and cost alerts, and incident review.
- **Reviews:** senior engineers review eval design in production-readiness reviews.

Leaders reinforce it by asking "what does the eval say?" in every review. See [06 AI / LLM](../../topics/06-ai-llm/ANSWERS.md) section E for evaluation methods.

#### How do you measure whether a forward deployed organisation is succeeding?

Use a small balanced set of metrics and review them monthly:

- **Customer outcomes:** share of engagements that reach production; measured business value delivered against agreed success criteria; customer satisfaction from sponsors.
- **Commercial impact:** net revenue retention and expansion on accounts with FDE involvement, deals where FDE work was decisive, renewal rate.
- **Speed and efficiency:** time to first value, time from kickoff to production, engineer-weeks per deployment, and how these fall over time as reusable assets grow.
- **Leverage:** percentage of deployments using reference architectures and shared components, number of field patterns adopted into the product.
- **Team health:** retention, engagement, hiring progress, utilisation (high enough to be useful, not so high there is no time to improve).

Avoid vanity measures such as hours billed or number of engagements started. Compare FDE-supported accounts with similar unsupported accounts where possible, and be honest that attribution is imperfect. The single most telling metric is usually whether customers keep using what the team built after the engineers leave.

#### Six customers each run a bespoke fork of the same integration. How do you fix it?

1. **Assess:** compare the forks; list common behaviour, real differences (customer-specific mappings, auth methods, data formats) and accidental differences (bug fixes applied to only some).
2. **Quantify the cost:** time spent patching each fork, incidents, upgrade delays. This builds the case for funding the work.
3. **Design one supported integration** with the differences moved into configuration or small extension points (mapping files, plugins, feature flags), plus contract tests for each customer's data shape.
4. **Agree ownership:** either the product team owns it as a product connector, or a field platform team owns it as a supported module with a versioning and release process.
5. **Migrate one customer at a time,** starting with the simplest; run old and new side by side where risk is high, compare outputs, then switch over. Communicate the benefit to each customer (faster fixes, upgrades).
6. **Prevent recurrence:** a rule that new customer needs extend the shared module, code review from its owners, and a catalogue of reusable components.

State the trade-off: the consolidation costs engineering time now and needs customer change windows, so sequence it with renewals and other planned work.

### Building and leading the team

#### How do you hire forward deployed engineers, and how would you design the interview loop?

Define the profile first: strong practical engineering, comfort with ambiguity, customer communication, ownership and learning speed. Different accounts may need different mixes (data-heavy, AI-heavy, integration-heavy).

**Loop design (each round tests something distinct, with a written rubric):**
1. Recruiter screen: motivation for customer-facing work, travel, communication.
2. Practical coding: realistic task (parse messy data, call an API, fix a bug), not puzzle-heavy.
3. Decomposition or case: an ambiguous customer problem; tests scoping and judgement.
4. System design in a customer context, including trade-offs and a non-technical explanation.
5. Customer role-play: a difficult stakeholder or scoping conversation.
6. Behavioral: ownership, failure, conflict.

**Practices:** trained interviewers, calibration sessions, independent written feedback before debrief, a hiring bar owned by the team rather than the most urgent account, and data on which signals predict success after hire. Source from adjacent roles (SE, consulting engineers, startup engineers). Sell the role honestly, including travel and context switching, so people who join want this work.

#### How do you structure a forward deployed team as it grows from 5 to 50 people?

- **About 5:** one flat team, a hands-on lead, generalists, shared on-call for escalations. Process is light; the lead writes the first playbooks.
- **About 15:** split into two or three pods aligned to segments, industries or regions, each with a tech lead. Introduce an engagement intake process, a shared reference library and a weekly field review.
- **About 30–50:** managers for pods (each managing roughly 6–8 people), staff or principal engineers working across pods on standards and the hardest accounts, and specialist roles where volume justifies them (evaluation, data integration, security). Possibly a small field platform team owning shared tools and connectors. Formal career ladder with IC and manager tracks, and partnerships with business-side roles such as deployment strategists.

Principles at every stage: keep pods aligned to customers so relationships are stable; keep the IC track as prestigious as the manager track; put someone in charge of reusable assets before duplication grows; and keep a direct, structured channel to product. Restructure only when a clear problem appears, and explain why.

#### How do you prevent burnout in a team that travels and is embedded with customers?

Burnout in field teams usually comes from too many accounts, unpredictable escalations, constant travel and unclear priorities.

- **Limit concurrency:** a cap on simultaneous engagements per engineer, enforced through staffing, not good intentions.
- **Predictable travel:** agreed on-site expectations per engagement, rotation for long-running accounts, and no surprise weekend travel without recovery time.
- **Protect from escalation load:** a rota for urgent issues so not everyone is interrupted, and clear severity definitions agreed with sales and support.
- **Give engineers the right to say no:** scope changes go through the lead, not straight to the engineer on site.
- **Rotation and growth:** time between engagements for reusable work, learning or product projects.
- **Watch signals:** working hours, travel days, one-to-one conversations, engagement survey results, and attrition; act early.
- **Lead by example:** managers who send messages at midnight set a norm.

Explain to the interviewer that this is also a business issue: losing an embedded engineer mid-engagement can put the account at risk.

#### How do you develop senior engineers into staff and principal engineers in a field organisation?

- **Make the expectations explicit:** a career ladder describing staff scope (outcomes across several engagements, reusable assets, technical direction) and principal scope (organisation-wide methodology, executive trust, product influence), with examples.
- **Give stretch scope deliberately:** lead a strategic account, own a reference architecture, run the evaluation standard, or lead a cross-team initiative.
- **Coach the new skills:** writing design documents and strategy memos, presenting to executives, influencing product, and choosing what not to do. Review their writing and bring them into executive meetings.
- **Sponsorship, not just mentorship:** put their name forward for visible work and credit them in leadership forums.
- **Feedback on the gap:** regular conversations comparing their work with the next level, with concrete examples.
- **Promotion based on demonstrated impact** over a sustained period, reviewed by a calibrated panel.

Also make clear that staying a strong senior engineer is a valid career; not everyone should be pushed towards staff level.

#### An engineer on your team is technically excellent but is damaging a customer relationship. What do you do?

1. **Get the facts quickly:** speak with the account team and, if appropriate, the customer contact; ask for specific examples (dismissive in meetings, ignoring requests, criticising their team).
2. **Talk to the engineer privately and soon:** describe the specific behaviour and its effect on the customer and the business, and listen; there may be context (unreasonable demands, burnout, a conflict with one person).
3. **Protect the customer:** if the relationship is badly damaged, adjust the engagement (another engineer leads customer-facing meetings, or the engineer moves to another account) without framing it as punishment to the customer.
4. **Agree a development plan:** clear expectations, coaching (for example rehearsing difficult conversations, pairing with a strong customer-facing colleague), and check-ins.
5. **Follow up:** if behaviour improves, recognise it; if not, escalate through the normal performance process.

Make the principle clear: in a customer-facing team, customer trust is part of technical excellence, not separate from it.

### Product and roadmap influence

#### How do you turn field feedback into roadmap changes the product team actually acts on?

Product teams ignore anecdotes and act on evidence presented in their terms.

- **One channel:** a single intake (a form or tracker) for field requests, rather than engineers messaging product managers directly.
- **Structured entries:** the problem (not the requested solution), affected customers, revenue at stake (pipeline, renewals), current workaround and its cost, and urgency.
- **Aggregate and rank:** the field leadership reviews requests monthly, merges duplicates and ranks the top ten by impact. Product receives a short, ranked list, not hundreds of tickets.
- **Regular forum:** a monthly session with product leadership to review the top items, with decisions recorded and explained back to the field.
- **Give product direct exposure:** invite product managers to customer workshops and escalations; nothing persuades like seeing the problem.
- **Do part of the work:** field prototypes or customer-validated designs reduce product's risk.
- **Close the loop:** tell customers and the field what was decided and why.

Track how many field-originated items ship and the revenue they affected; this builds credibility for the next request.

#### Product leadership refuses a feature your three largest customers need. What do you do?

1. **Understand their reasoning:** strategy, cost, technical risk, or lack of evidence. They may be right; three customers can still be a niche.
2. **Strengthen the evidence:** revenue at stake, renewal dates, whether other customers or prospects have the same need, and the cost of the workaround. Describe the underlying problem, not one specific feature.
3. **Look for a different solution:** an extension point or API that lets the field build it outside the core, a smaller version, or a configuration option.
4. **Escalate properly if the stakes are high:** a joint written decision for the executive team setting out both views, the trade-offs and the business impact, rather than going around product.
5. **Accept the decision and manage the customers:** tell them honestly what will and will not happen, offer the workaround or a partner solution, and avoid promising future dates.

Show the interviewer you respect product's ownership of the roadmap while making sure the decision is made with full information. "Disagree and commit" applies to the field too.

#### When should the field build something the product team has not prioritised?

Build in the field when all of these are true:

- **There is a clear customer outcome** that matters now (a deal, a renewal, a deployment blocked) and product cannot deliver in time.
- **It can sit outside the core:** on public APIs or documented extension points, so it does not fork the product or create hidden obligations for product engineering.
- **Ownership is agreed:** who maintains it, for how long, and what happens on product upgrades. The customer knows whether it is supported.
- **It is a learning opportunity:** the build tests whether the need is general, and its usage data can inform product.

Do not build when it duplicates something on the near-term roadmap, requires changes to core internals, or would create a support commitment nobody can fund. Treat field builds as candidates for product: track usage, and when the pattern repeats, hand a validated design to the product team. Tell product before building, not after; surprises damage trust.

### Commercial and cross-functional leadership

#### Sales leadership wants to promise a capability that does not exist yet. How do you handle it?

- **Understand the deal:** what the customer actually needs, whether it is a must-have or a nice-to-have, and when they need it.
- **Find a truthful offer:** often the need can be met with existing capabilities, a configuration, a partner, or a field build on public APIs. Propose that.
- **If it is on the roadmap:** share it only through the approved process (product-confirmed, with appropriate caveats and no contractual dates unless product agrees).
- **If it does not exist and is not planned:** say so clearly to sales leadership and explain the risk: a failed deployment, a damaged reference, possible contractual liability and a hard renewal.
- **Agree a rule for the future** with sales and product leadership: who can commit to roadmap items, and how.

Make the commercial case for honesty: customers who are oversold churn and talk to their peers. A good field leader helps sales win by finding what is true and still compelling, not by saying no to everything. Escalate jointly if you cannot agree, rather than undermining the sales team in front of the customer.

#### How do you scope and price a paid pilot or a forward deployed engagement?

**Scope:**
- A specific problem, defined users and data, written success criteria with an evaluation method, and a fixed duration (often four to twelve weeks).
- Clear customer obligations: an executive sponsor, a named owner, data and system access by specific dates, security review timing.
- Explicit exclusions and what happens at the end (conversion to a production contract if criteria are met).

**Price:** depends on company strategy.
- Many companies charge for pilots so the customer is committed, often crediting the fee against the production contract.
- Price by estimated effort (engineer-weeks multiplied by a rate) and by value to the customer, then adjust for strategic importance.
- Some engagements are bundled into a platform deal or offered as a fixed-fee package (for example a standard "AI launch" offer).

**Avoid:** unlimited free pilots, vague success criteria and open-ended embedded engineers, because they consume capacity without a decision. The point of a pilot is a clear decision at the end, so agree in advance who decides and when.

#### How do you balance pre-sales demands against post-sale delivery with a fixed team?

- **Make the trade-off visible:** track how capacity is split between pre-sales support, active deployments and escalations, and share it with sales and customer success leaders.
- **Agree an allocation policy:** for example a fixed share of capacity for pre-sales, with priority to deals above a size threshold or in a strategic segment; the rest protected for delivery.
- **Qualify pre-sales requests:** a technical deep dive or custom demo is offered only when the deal is qualified (budget, sponsor, timeline), otherwise a standard demo or solutions engineer handles it.
- **Reuse assets:** standard demos, reference architectures and recorded walkthroughs cut pre-sales effort.
- **Protect deployments in flight:** pulling engineers off live deployments damages current customers, so treat that as an exception decided at leadership level.
- **Use the data to argue for headcount** or for building a separate pre-sales function when demand is sustained.

The goal is that every hour goes to the work with the highest expected long-term value, and that sales leaders understand and agree with the rules.

### Technical depth at principal level

#### Whiteboard: design the reference architecture for an LLM agent platform shared by a regulated enterprise's business units.

Clarify: number of business units and use cases, data sensitivity, regulators, cloud and model constraints, existing identity and data platforms.

**Core layers:**
- **Access:** an internal AI gateway in front of approved models (hosted APIs under enterprise terms and optionally self-hosted open-weight models), handling authentication, routing, rate limits, cost attribution per business unit, and logging.
- **Guardrails:** input and output filtering (sensitive data detection and redaction, prompt-injection checks, policy checks), configurable per use case.
- **Knowledge:** a governed retrieval service with document-level access control inherited from source systems, data classification, and lineage.
- **Tools:** a registry of approved tools and connectors (for example via a standard tool protocol) with scoped permissions, human approval for high-impact actions, and audit trails.
- **Agent runtime:** templates for common patterns, state management, timeouts and step limits.
- **Evaluation and monitoring:** shared eval harness, pre-release gates, trace capture, quality, cost and latency dashboards, incident process.
- **Governance:** use-case registration with risk tiering, model inventory, approval workflow, data residency and retention.

**Operating model:** a central platform team owns the shared layers; business units build use cases on templates. Discuss trade-offs: centralisation versus speed, and gateway latency.

#### How do you stay technically credible while spending most of your time with executives?

- **Keep one hands-on thread:** build a prototype or demo each quarter, review real code and designs, or join a production incident review regularly.
- **Go deep on the hardest escalations,** not every escalation. Those teach the most about where the product and deployments break.
- **Read primary sources:** model and platform release notes, papers and engineering blogs relevant to customers, and the team's design documents.
- **Learn from the team:** short technical sessions where engineers present what they built; ask detailed questions.
- **Use the product yourself** the way a customer would, including setup and failure cases.
- **Be honest about limits:** say "I will check with the engineer who built it" instead of improvising; credibility is lost faster by bluffing than by deferring.

In the interview, give concrete examples of recent hands-on work and be ready for a technical question at any depth. Many companies hiring for these roles test exactly this, sometimes with a coding or design round.

#### How do you make and communicate a high-stakes technical decision with incomplete information?

1. **Frame the decision:** what exactly must be decided, by when, and what happens if we wait. Distinguish reversible decisions (decide fast) from hard-to-reverse ones (invest more in analysis).
2. **Gather the information that changes the answer,** within the time available: a short spike or benchmark, customer input, expert opinions. Stop when more information will not change the decision.
3. **Lay out the options** with trade-offs, risks and costs, and the assumptions each depends on.
4. **Decide, and write it down:** an architecture decision record covering context, options, decision, rationale, risks and the signals that would make us revisit it.
5. **Communicate by audience:** executives get the decision, the business impact and the risk; engineers get the technical rationale; the customer gets what it means for them.
6. **Set a review point** and monitor the signals; change course openly if the evidence changes.

The key message: senior leaders are judged on the quality of the decision process, not only on outcomes, and on how quickly they correct a wrong decision.

### Leadership behavioral

#### Tell me about a time you set technical direction across several teams without formal authority.

**What they look for:** you identified a cross-team problem, built agreement through evidence and relationships rather than position, made it easy to adopt, and the change stuck.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [Several teams or customers solving the same problem differently; the cost of that]
- **T:** [What you set out to change; why you had no authority to mandate it]
- **A:** [How you gathered evidence, wrote a proposal or design document, consulted the teams, built a working example, handled objections, and got a sponsor]
- **R:** [Adoption across teams, measurable effect on time, cost or quality; what remained]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "Three teams were integrating with external partners, each with its own retry and authentication approach, and incidents kept repeating. I wrote a two-page proposal for a shared integration pattern, built a reference implementation with one team, and invited the other leads to review it before I presented it to engineering leadership. One lead objected because it slowed their release, so I added a migration path that let them adopt it gradually. Within two quarters all three teams used it, and partner-related incidents fell noticeably."

#### Tell me about a time you turned around a failing customer engagement.

**What they look for:** honest diagnosis, ownership without blaming the customer or colleagues, a reset with the customer's executives, a focused recovery plan and a measurable result, plus what you changed so it does not repeat.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The engagement, how it was failing (missed milestones, unhappy sponsor, low adoption), what was at stake]
- **T:** [Your role in the recovery]
- **A:** [How you found the root causes, the conversation with the sponsor, the reset plan (reduced scope, new success criteria, staffing changes), how you communicated progress]
- **R:** [Outcome: live in production, renewal, sponsor feedback; what you changed in the methodology]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "A deployment was three months late and the sponsor had stopped attending reviews. I interviewed the team and the customer's users and found we were building for requirements set by a manager who had left. I met the sponsor, acknowledged our part, and proposed cutting scope to one workflow with clear success criteria in six weeks. We delivered it, adoption grew, the sponsor renewed, and we added a stakeholder-change check to our engagement reviews."

#### Tell me about a time you changed a product's direction based on what you saw in the field.

**What they look for:** you spotted a pattern rather than one customer's request, built a data-backed case, worked with product rather than around it, and can point to the change and its effect.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [What you saw across customers or users]
- **T:** [Why it mattered to the business; who owned the decision]
- **A:** [Evidence you gathered (customers affected, revenue, workaround cost), how you framed the problem for product, prototypes or pilots you ran]
- **R:** [What changed in the product, adoption or revenue effect, how it changed the relationship between field and product]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "Across several enterprise deployments, customers rebuilt the same approval workflow on top of our API. I collected the implementations, estimated the engineering time customers spent, and showed product leadership that it was delaying go-lives. I brought a product manager to two customer sessions and shared a field prototype. The team built a native version the next quarter, and deployment time for new customers in that segment dropped."

#### Tell me about a hiring or performance decision you got wrong and what you changed afterwards.

**What they look for:** honest ownership of a real mistake, how you handled the person fairly, and a concrete change to your process. Avoid stories where the mistake was really someone else's.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The hire or decision, and what you missed or misjudged]
- **T:** [What needed to happen once it was clear]
- **A:** [How you addressed it with the person (feedback, support, timeline), how you protected the team and customers, how you reviewed your own process]
- **R:** [Outcome for the person and team; the specific change you made to hiring or management (new interview signal, earlier feedback, clearer expectations)]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "I hired a strong engineer into a customer-facing role based mainly on technical rounds. Within weeks, customers found them hard to work with. I gave specific feedback early, paired them with a senior colleague and adjusted their account. It improved somewhat, but they were happier in an internal platform role, and we arranged that move. I added a customer role-play round to our loop and changed my rule: I no longer let one strong signal outweigh a missing one."

#### Tell me about a time you told a senior executive something they did not want to hear.

**What they look for:** courage combined with judgement: you had evidence, you chose the right time and setting, you offered options, and you preserved the relationship.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The executive, the plan or belief, and why it was a problem]
- **T:** [Why it was your responsibility to raise it]
- **A:** [How you prepared the evidence, where and how you raised it (privately, early), the options you proposed, how you handled their reaction]
- **R:** [The decision that followed, the business effect, and the relationship afterwards]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "A customer's chief operating officer planned to remove human review from an AI-assisted process at launch to show savings quickly. Our evaluation showed an error rate that was too high for some case types. I asked for a private meeting, showed examples of the errors and their cost, and proposed automating the low-risk case types first while keeping review for the rest. They were frustrated at first but agreed. The phased launch avoided a visible failure, and they later asked us to run the evaluation for the next phase."

## Prep checklist

1. Write a one-page leadership narrative: the scope you have owned (teams, systems, customers, budgets), the results, and why this role is the logical next step. Make every number accurate and checkable, because senior hires are reference-checked closely.
2. Prepare eight to ten leadership stories (influence without authority, turnaround, hiring, performance, product influence, executive disagreement, failure, strategy), each with scope and a business result; use [10 Behavioral](../../topics/10-behavioral/ANSWERS.md) and your own story bank.
3. Build and rehearse a 20-minute executive presentation (an AI strategy or a past engagement), with an answer-first structure, and practise with someone who interrupts with CFO, CISO and COO questions.
4. Write a sample multi-quarter account plan and a 90-day plan for the role you are interviewing for, based on public information about the company.
5. Draft your deployment methodology on one page (stages, gates, artefacts, metrics) and your view on product versus custom work.
6. Refresh technical depth: RAG, agents, evaluation, cost and security in [06 AI / LLM](../../topics/06-ai-llm/ANSWERS.md), and practise one enterprise platform design from [03 System design](../../topics/03-system-design/ANSWERS.md) out loud.
7. Do at least two timed coding sessions if the company's loop includes coding at senior levels (common at AI labs).
8. Research the company's field organisation: public talks by its field leaders, job postings for the team you would lead or join, and its customer case studies.
9. Prepare sharp questions for each interviewer about charter, metrics, headcount, the product relationship and decision rights, using [14 Questions to ask](../../topics/14-questions-to-ask/ANSWERS.md).
10. Prepare for compensation and level discussions: know your target level and the scope that justifies it.
