# Technology / AI Consultant (consulting firms and systems integrators)

A client-facing adviser and delivery lead at a consulting firm or systems integrator who structures business problems, shapes AI strategies and roadmaps, runs workshops, writes proposals, and leads delivery teams (often onshore and offshore) that build and implement the solutions.

## The role

**Day to day.** Work is organised into client engagements ("projects" or "cases") that last from a few weeks to many months:

- **Problem structuring and analysis:** breaking a client question into parts, forming hypotheses, gathering data through interviews and analysis, and synthesising findings into recommendations.
- **AI strategy and roadmaps:** use-case discovery and prioritisation, readiness assessments, build-versus-buy advice, operating model and governance design, and business cases.
- **Workshops and stakeholder management:** facilitating sessions with client executives and teams, managing a steering committee, and handling disagreement between client functions.
- **Solution design and delivery:** architecture, vendor and platform selection, and leading delivery teams that build prototypes and production systems, frequently with offshore delivery centres.
- **Selling and proposals:** at senior levels, writing proposals and statements of work, responding to RFPs, and estimating effort and price.
- **Practice building:** creating reusable assets (accelerators, frameworks, demos), thought leadership, training colleagues, and recruiting.

**Common titles / aliases.** Consultant, Senior Consultant, Manager, Senior Manager, Principal, Associate Partner / Director, Partner. Technology-specific titles include Technology Consultant, AI Consultant, Data and AI Consultant, Solution Architect (consulting), Technical Architect, Engagement Manager, Delivery Manager. At strategy firms' technology arms you may see Data Scientist, Data Engineer, Machine Learning Engineer, Product Manager or "Lead Consultant". Level names differ between firms, so map them by years of experience and scope rather than by title.

**Firms that hire for it (examples).** Strategy firms with technology and AI arms: McKinsey (QuantumBlack, AI by McKinsey), BCG (BCG X), Bain. Large professional services and systems integrators: Accenture, Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG, Capgemini, IBM Consulting. Technology consultancies: Thoughtworks, Slalom, EPAM. India-headquartered IT services firms with digital and AI practices: Infosys, TCS, Wipro, HCLTech, Tech Mahindra. Cloud and AI vendors' partner ecosystems also hire through these firms. Many of these firms run large delivery centres in India.

**How it differs from FDE.**

| | Technology / AI Consultant | Forward Deployed Engineer |
|---|---|---|
| Employer's product | Expertise and people (billable time or fixed-price outcomes) | A software platform |
| Neutrality | Usually advises across vendors (though many firms have strong vendor alliances) | Deploys its own company's product |
| Main output | Recommendations, roadmaps, designs, and delivered projects | Production deployments on the product |
| Commercial model | Utilisation, project margin, follow-on work | Product revenue, retention, expansion |
| Interview core | Case interviews, structured problem solving, behavioral fit, plus technical rounds for technical tracks | Coding, system design, decomposition, customer scenarios |
| Team | Pyramid of consultants, often onshore plus offshore | Small engineering teams |

**Who it suits.** People who like variety across industries, structured thinking, writing and presenting, and leading teams; who are comfortable with a commercial model based on billable time; and who are happy to be judged on client impact rather than on owning a product long term.

**Career paths.** Up the consulting ladder to partner; specialist roles (chief architect, AI practice lead); moving to industry as a head of AI, digital or data, or as a CTO or transformation leader; product and strategy roles at AI vendors (deployment strategist, solutions architect, field CTO); or founding a startup.

## Model answers

### Role and firm fit

#### Why consulting rather than a product company, and why this firm?

**What they look for:** genuine reasons that match what consulting offers (variety, pace of learning, client impact, structured development, exposure to senior leaders), honesty about the trade-offs, and specific knowledge of this firm.

**Structure:**
- **Why consulting:** "I want to work on [type of problem] across several industries and clients, and I learn fastest when I face new contexts. I have enjoyed the parts of my work that are closest to consulting: [structuring an ambiguous problem, a business case, leading a cross-functional delivery, presenting to senior stakeholders]."
- **What you bring:** "I have built and delivered [systems] myself, so I can design recommendations that are realistic to implement, which many AI strategies are not."
- **Why this firm:** something specific: its AI practice and how it combines strategy and build, industries it is strong in, a published piece of thought leadership you read, people you spoke to, its training or delivery model.
- **Show you understand the trade-offs:** travel, client-driven schedules, utilisation targets, and less long-term ownership of one product. Say why you accept them.

Avoid "prestige" or "exit options" as your main reason, and avoid generic praise that could apply to any firm.

#### How is a technology consultant different from a forward deployed engineer?

- **Employer and incentives:** a consultant sells expertise and people; revenue comes from fees for time or outcomes. An FDE works for a software company and is measured on the success and growth of its product at customers.
- **Neutrality:** consultants usually advise across vendors and can recommend any platform, although large firms have strategic alliances with cloud and AI vendors. An FDE deploys their own company's product.
- **Output:** consultants produce strategies, roadmaps, designs and delivered projects that the client then owns. FDEs produce running deployments on their platform and feed lessons into the product.
- **Team shape:** consulting teams are a pyramid (partner, manager, consultants, analysts, often with offshore delivery teams); FDE teams are small groups of senior engineers.
- **Skills emphasis:** consulting puts more weight on structured problem solving, slides and documents, workshops, estimation and commercial skills; FDE puts more weight on hands-on coding.

The roles are converging: consulting firms increasingly build AI solutions, and AI vendors hire people with consulting skills. Say which parts of each you enjoy.

#### How does a consulting firm make money, and what do utilisation and leverage mean for you?

- **Revenue:** fees from client engagements, priced as time and materials (rate multiplied by hours), fixed price for a defined scope, or occasionally outcome-based. Large firms also earn from managed services and long-running outsourcing contracts.
- **Utilisation:** the share of available working time that is billed to clients. Firms set targets by level; junior staff have higher targets, partners spend more time selling. Low utilisation hurts margin, so staffing on projects matters.
- **Leverage:** the ratio of junior to senior staff on engagements. A partner and a manager can lead a team of many consultants, so the firm earns on the team's time, not only on senior time. Offshore delivery centres increase leverage at lower cost.
- **Margin:** revenue minus delivery cost (mostly salaries). Fixed-price work raises the risk: overruns come out of margin.
- **Follow-on work:** most growth comes from existing clients, so delivery quality drives sales.

What it means for you: aim for good utilisation on valued work, estimate accurately, protect margin by managing scope, and help create follow-on work by delivering well. At manager level and above, you will be measured partly on sales and margin.

### Case interviews and structured problem solving

#### How do you approach a case interview from start to finish?

1. **Listen and take notes;** play back the question and the objective in one sentence ("The goal is to decide whether to... measured by...").
2. **Ask clarifying questions** about the objective, scope, timeline and any constraints. Keep it to two or three questions.
3. **Take a short pause to structure,** then present a MECE structure (an issue tree tailored to the case, not a memorised framework) and say where you would start and why.
4. **State a hypothesis** early and use it to prioritise analysis.
5. **Drive the analysis:** ask for data, interpret exhibits (state the "so what"), do maths out loud with clear units and sanity checks.
6. **Brainstorm** in a structured way when asked (group ideas into categories).
7. **Synthesise:** a recommendation first, two or three supporting reasons, risks, and next steps.

Two common formats: candidate-led (you drive, common at BCG and Bain) and interviewer-led (the interviewer asks a series of questions, common at McKinsey). Practise both. Throughout, think aloud, stay calm with numbers, and connect every step back to the client's objective.

#### What does MECE mean, and how do you build an issue tree?

**MECE** means "mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive": the parts of a breakdown do not overlap, and together they cover the whole problem. It prevents double counting and missed areas.

**Building an issue tree:**
1. Start with the key question in one sentence ("How can the client raise profit by 10% in two years?").
2. Break it into two to five branches using a logical split: a formula (profit = revenue minus cost; revenue = volume times price), a process (stages of a customer journey), or segments (customers, products, regions), or internal versus external.
3. Break each branch further until each leaf can be tested with specific data or analysis.
4. Check MECE at each level: could an item belong to two branches? Is anything missing?

There are two types: **issue trees** (what are the possible drivers) and **hypothesis trees** (what must be true for our answer to hold). Example for AI in customer service: reduce volume of contacts, reduce cost per contact (handle time, channel mix, automation), and improve quality (resolution rate, satisfaction). Each branch leads to measurable analyses.

#### What is a hypothesis-driven approach, and what are its risks?

**The approach:** after an initial look at the problem, state the most likely answer early ("We believe call-centre cost is rising mainly because contact volume has grown from digital channel failures"), then design the analyses that would prove or disprove it. It focuses limited time on the work that matters and gives a storyline from the start.

**Steps:** form a hypothesis from facts and experience; list what must be true for it to hold; identify the analyses and data for each; run them in order of importance; revise or drop the hypothesis as evidence comes in.

**Risks:**
- **Confirmation bias:** looking only for supporting evidence. Mitigate by explicitly listing what would disprove it and testing that first.
- **Anchoring too early** on a wrong hypothesis and missing the real problem. Keep a few alternatives alive.
- **Client perception:** a hypothesis stated too confidently can sound like a conclusion reached before listening. Present it as a working assumption.

In AI work, the equivalent is defining success criteria and an evaluation set before building, then letting the measurements change the plan.

#### How do you synthesise findings into a recommendation using the pyramid principle?

The pyramid principle (from Barbara Minto's work) says: **lead with the answer, then group supporting arguments, then the evidence.**

1. **Governing thought:** the recommendation in one sentence, ideally answering the client's question directly. "Deploy an AI assistant for agents first, not a customer-facing chatbot, because it delivers most of the savings with much lower risk."
2. **Key supporting points:** three or so, MECE, each a full sentence that makes a claim (not a topic label): "Agent handle time is the biggest cost driver", "Agent-assist reaches production in three months", "Customer-facing automation needs data and controls the bank does not yet have."
3. **Evidence under each point:** data, analysis, interviews, benchmarks.
4. **Then:** risks, next steps and decisions required.

Use the same structure for slides (action titles that read as a story when read in order), emails and executive summaries. Also use **SCQA** (situation, complication, question, answer) to open a document. In a case interview, the final synthesis should take about a minute and follow this exact order.

#### Case: a retail bank's contact-centre costs have risen sharply in two years, and the CEO asks whether generative AI can help. How do you approach it?

**Clarify:** the size of the increase, channels (voice, chat, email), whether costs rose because of volume or cost per contact, constraints (regulation, customer experience targets), and the timeline.

**Structure:** cost = contact volume x cost per contact.
- **Volume:** why customers contact the bank (top reasons); avoidable contacts caused by failures (for example failed digital journeys or unclear communications). AI can help with self-service, but fixing root causes may beat automating them.
- **Cost per contact:** handle time (agent-assist that summarises, finds answers and drafts replies), after-call work (automatic summaries), channel mix (move to cheaper channels), and staffing and attrition (onboarding time cut by AI coaching).
- **Quality and risk:** resolution rate, complaints, regulatory conduct risk, data protection.

**Hypothesis:** most of the increase is volume from a few avoidable contact reasons, and agent-assist plus summarisation is the lowest-risk, fastest AI win.

**Analysis:** contact-reason breakdown, handle-time components, benchmark and pilot data on assist tools.

**Recommendation format:** fix the top root causes, pilot agent-assist and summarisation with a control group, then expand to customer-facing automation for simple, low-risk intents once quality is proven.

#### Case: a manufacturer wants to use AI to reduce unplanned equipment downtime. Structure the problem.

**Clarify:** which plants and equipment, current downtime and its cost (lost output, overtime, scrap), current maintenance approach (reactive, scheduled, condition-based), data available (sensors, maintenance logs), and the goal.

**Structure: downtime = number of failures x time to recover.**
- **Fewer failures:** predictive maintenance (models on sensor data that predict failures), better scheduled maintenance, root-cause analysis of repeat failures, operator practices.
- **Faster recovery:** diagnosis speed (an LLM assistant over manuals and maintenance history for technicians), spare parts availability, technician scheduling.
- **Enablers:** sensor coverage and data quality, data platform, integration with the maintenance management system, skills, and change management on the shop floor.

**Prioritise:** use a Pareto analysis: usually a small set of assets causes most downtime cost. Start there.

**Feasibility check for prediction:** enough failure history for those assets? If failures are rare, start with anomaly detection or condition monitoring rather than supervised prediction.

**Recommendation shape:** a pilot on the top critical assets with a baseline, success metrics (fewer unplanned stops, mean time to repair), and a plan to scale across plants if it works. Mention the value: avoided downtime hours multiplied by cost per hour.

#### Case: an insurer takes 20 days on average to settle a claim. Where would you start?

**Clarify:** which product lines, the target (industry peers, customer expectations), whether the goal is speed, cost, customer satisfaction or fraud control, and how the 20 days is distributed (an average can hide a long tail).

**Start with the process and data:** map the claim journey (first notice of loss, documentation, assessment, approval, payment) and measure time in each stage, split into working time and waiting time. Typically most of the elapsed time is waiting (for documents, for an assessor, for approvals, in handoffs).

**Segment claims:** simple and low-value claims versus complex or suspicious ones. Simple claims can often be fast-tracked.

**Levers:**
- **Process:** straight-through processing rules for simple claims, fewer handoffs, approval limits.
- **AI:** document extraction and classification (forms, invoices, reports) with LLMs or document AI, triage models that route claims, fraud scoring, assistant tools for adjusters, and automated customer updates.
- **Risk controls:** human review for high-value or low-confidence cases, auditability for regulators.

**Hypothesis:** waiting time on documents and manual triage drives most of the delay; automating intake and triage could cut the cycle substantially for simple claims. Confirm with data before recommending.

### Estimation and quantitative

#### Market sizing: estimate the annual market for paid AI coding assistants among professional software developers in India.

State the approach, then the assumptions, and keep the numbers round. All figures below are illustrative assumptions for practice, not researched data; in an interview, say so and invite correction.

**Bottom-up:**
1. **Professional developers in India:** assume about 5 million.
2. **Share whose employer or who themselves pay for an AI coding assistant:** split by segment. Assume IT services and global capability centres employ most developers and adopt fastest. Assume 30% paid adoption overall in the near term = 1.5 million paid seats.
3. **Price per seat:** business plans are typically priced per user per month. Assume an average of about USD 20 per month after enterprise discounts = USD 240 per year.
4. **Market:** 1.5 million x USD 240 = about USD 360 million per year.

**Sanity checks:** compare with the size of the developer tools market, and run a range: 15% adoption and USD 15 gives about USD 135 million; 50% and USD 25 gives about USD 750 million.

**Then discuss drivers:** enterprise policies on code privacy, price competition and bundling with cloud or IDE subscriptions, free tiers, and usage-based pricing for agentic tools. Ending with a range and key drivers shows judgement, not just arithmetic.

#### Estimate the annual savings from automating 40% of a bank's customer emails.

Clarify what "automating" means (fully resolved without an agent, or drafted for an agent to approve) and the bank's size. Then build it step by step; numbers are illustrative assumptions.

1. **Volume:** assume 200,000 customer emails per month.
2. **Automated share:** 40% = 80,000 emails per month.
3. **Agent time per email today:** assume 6 minutes including reading, looking up systems and replying = 8,000 agent hours per month.
4. **Loaded cost per agent hour:** depends heavily on location; assume USD 15 (blended onshore and offshore) = USD 120,000 per month, about USD 1.44 million per year gross.
5. **Subtract costs and leakage:** quality review of a sample (say 10% of automated emails, at the same handling time), reopened cases where automation fails, platform and model usage, and support and maintenance. Assume these total 25–35% of gross.
6. **Net:** roughly USD 0.9–1.1 million per year.

**Key caveats:** savings are only real if agent capacity is reduced through attrition or redeployed to valuable work; customer satisfaction and complaint rates must be protected; regulated replies may need human approval. Present a range and the two or three assumptions that matter most.

#### Estimate the monthly LLM cost of an internal knowledge assistant for 20,000 employees.

Prices change often, so state that you are using illustrative per-token prices and would check current list prices.

1. **Active users:** assume 30% use it on a given working day = 6,000 users.
2. **Queries:** 5 per active user per day = 30,000 per day; about 22 working days = 660,000 queries per month.
3. **Tokens per query (RAG):** system prompt plus retrieved passages plus question, assume 3,000 input tokens; answer 500 output tokens.
4. **Monthly tokens:** input 660,000 x 3,000 = about 2 billion; output 660,000 x 500 = 330 million.
5. **Illustrative prices:** USD 3 per million input tokens and USD 15 per million output tokens for a mid-tier model.
6. **Model cost:** input about USD 5,900 + output about USD 5,000 = about USD 11,000 per month.

**Add:** embeddings for indexing and queries (usually small), vector database and hosting, logging and evaluation runs, plus engineering and support (often larger than token cost).

**Levers to reduce cost:** route simple questions to a smaller model, prompt caching for the fixed system prompt, fewer and shorter retrieved passages, response length limits, and caching common answers. Conclude that model cost is modest relative to the value if it saves each active user even a few minutes a day.

#### How do you estimate effort and team size for an AI implementation project?

Combine a bottom-up estimate with a top-down check, and be explicit about uncertainty.

1. **Break the scope into work packages:** discovery and design, data access and preparation, integrations, core AI components (retrieval, prompts, agents, model selection), evaluation set and harness, user interface, security and compliance reviews, testing, deployment and monitoring, change management and training, hypercare.
2. **Estimate each package** in person-days with ranges, ideally with the people who will do the work, using similar past projects as references.
3. **Add AI-specific contingency:** quality iteration is uncertain, so time-box experimentation and plan go/no-go points; data access and security approvals are frequent causes of delay.
4. **Add management and overhead:** project management, client meetings, steering committees, documentation (often 15–20%).
5. **Shape the team:** roles needed (engagement lead, solution architect, data engineer, AI engineers, QA, change lead), onshore versus offshore split, and the schedule implied by dependencies.
6. **Top-down check:** compare with similar delivered projects and with the client's budget.

Present it as phases with a firm estimate for the next phase and a range for later ones, and list the assumptions that would change the estimate.

### AI strategy and roadmap

#### A client asks for an AI strategy. What does a good one contain?

A good AI strategy is a business strategy with AI as the means, and it leads to decisions and funding, not just a vision.

1. **Ambition linked to business goals:** where AI should create value (growth, cost, risk, customer experience), with measurable targets.
2. **Prioritised use-case portfolio:** a small number of high-value, feasible use cases, balanced between quick wins and larger bets, with business cases.
3. **Foundations:** data (quality, access, governance), technology platform (models, cloud, tooling, security), and integration with core systems.
4. **People and skills:** talent plan, training for leaders and staff, roles such as product owners and AI engineers.
5. **Operating model and governance:** who decides, funds and builds; a centre of excellence or federated model; risk management and responsible AI policy.
6. **Partners and sourcing:** build, buy or partner decisions and vendor strategy.
7. **Roadmap and funding:** phased plan over 12 to 36 months with gates, budget and ownership.
8. **Measurement:** KPIs, value tracking and review cadence.

Warn against the common failure: a long list of ideas and a vision slide with no owners, no foundations and no funding.

#### How do you prioritise a long list of AI use cases?

1. **Normalise the list:** describe each use case consistently (business problem, users, process, data needed, expected benefit). Merge duplicates.
2. **Score on two axes:**
   - **Value:** financial impact, strategic importance, number of users affected, and risk reduction.
   - **Feasibility:** data availability and quality, technical complexity, integration effort, regulatory and ethical risk, organisational readiness (a willing business owner), and time to value.
3. **Plot a value-feasibility matrix:** start with high-value, high-feasibility use cases; plan foundations for high-value, low-feasibility ones; drop low-value ones.
4. **Consider sequencing and reuse:** a use case that builds a shared capability (a document pipeline, a knowledge base, security approval) can unlock several others.
5. **Check risk tier:** high-impact decisions about people (credit, hiring, healthcare) need stronger controls and may come later.
6. **Validate with stakeholders** in a workshop and agree the top three to five, each with an owner and success measure.

Be transparent about scoring weights, agree them with the client before scoring, and treat scores as an input to discussion, not a mechanical answer.

#### How do you assess an organisation's AI readiness?

Assess across dimensions, with evidence rather than self-reported opinions, and score each on a simple maturity scale (for example 1 to 5).

- **Strategy and leadership:** is there a clear ambition, an executive owner and funding?
- **Data:** availability, quality, accessibility, governance, ownership, and whether key data is digitised and integrated.
- **Technology:** cloud platform, approved AI tools and models, MLOps or LLMOps practices, security and identity integration.
- **People and skills:** technical talent, AI literacy among business leaders and staff, and capacity for change.
- **Processes and operating model:** how use cases are funded, built and scaled; collaboration between business and IT.
- **Governance and risk:** responsible AI policies, model risk management, data protection compliance, and incident handling.
- **Culture:** willingness to experiment, trust in data, and incentives.

**Methods:** interviews across levels, a survey, document review, and a look at actual projects. **Output:** a heat map of current versus target maturity, the main gaps, and the actions that most limit progress. Tie it to the use cases that matter; readiness only matters for what the client wants to do.

#### How do you advise a client on build, buy or partner for generative AI?

Decide per use case, not once for the whole company.

- **Buy (off-the-shelf AI products or features in existing software):** for common, non-differentiating needs such as meeting notes, coding assistants, or AI features in CRM and productivity suites. Fast and lower risk; limited customisation and vendor lock-in.
- **Build (on foundation models via APIs or open-weight models, using the client's data and workflows):** for use cases that differentiate the business or need deep integration with proprietary data and processes. More control; requires skills, platform and ongoing maintenance.
- **Partner (a specialist vendor or an implementation partner, sometimes co-development):** when the capability is specialised (for example document AI in a specific industry) or the client lacks skills now but wants them later.

**Criteria:** strategic differentiation, time to value, total cost of ownership (licences, usage, build and run), data sensitivity and residency, integration needs, internal skills, vendor maturity and exit options.

Recommend an architecture that keeps options open: a model-agnostic layer (gateway) so models can be swapped, and contracts that address data use and portability. As a consultant, disclose any alliance relationships your firm has with vendors.

#### How do you design an AI operating model and a centre of excellence?

**Choose the model to match the organisation's maturity:**
- **Centralised:** a central AI team builds for the business. Good early on for standards and scarce skills; can become a bottleneck.
- **Federated (hub and spoke):** a central centre of excellence (CoE) sets standards and provides platforms; business units have their own AI teams building use cases. Common in larger, more mature organisations.
- **Decentralised:** business units act independently. Fast, but leads to duplication and inconsistent risk control.

**What the CoE does:** strategy and portfolio management, shared platform and tools, standards (architecture, security, evaluation), responsible AI and risk governance, reusable assets, training and community, and vendor management.

**Key design elements:** decision rights (who approves use cases and funding), funding model (central budget versus business-unit funding), roles (product owners, AI engineers, data engineers, risk partners), intake and prioritisation process, and value tracking.

**Evolution:** start centralised or as a strong hub, then federate as business units build skills. Define success measures for the CoE (use cases in production, value delivered, reuse) so it does not become a research group with no impact.

#### The client has run twenty AI pilots and none is in production. What do you do?

This is often called "pilot purgatory". Diagnose before prescribing.

**Diagnose (two to four weeks):** review each pilot: business owner, success criteria, results, and why it stopped. Common causes: no business owner, no clear value case, pilots chosen for novelty, poor data, no production platform or security path, no budget for scaling, no change management.

**Then act:**
1. **Stop or pause most pilots** and pick two or three with the strongest value and an engaged owner.
2. **Define production criteria** up front: success metrics, evaluation thresholds, security and compliance requirements, operating owner.
3. **Build the missing foundations once:** an approved platform, a security and risk approval process, monitoring and support, so each use case does not start from zero.
4. **Fund scaling, not just experiments:** a governance forum with a budget and stage gates.
5. **Manage adoption:** process redesign, training and incentives; measure usage and outcomes.
6. **Publicise the first production success** with measured value to build momentum.

Going forward, require every new pilot to have a sponsor, a value hypothesis and a defined path to production before it starts.

#### How do you build responsible AI, regulation and data protection into a client's roadmap?

Make it part of the operating model from the start rather than a final review.

- **Principles and policy:** agree a short set of principles (fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, safety, human oversight) and turn them into practical policies and standards.
- **Risk tiering:** classify each use case by risk (for example an internal summary tool is low; credit decisions or medical triage are high) and apply proportionate controls. This aligns with the risk-based approach of the EU AI Act and with frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001.
- **Data protection:** lawful basis and consent, data minimisation, retention, cross-border transfer and residency rules under the relevant laws (for example GDPR in Europe, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act in India), and vendor terms on data use.
- **Controls in the lifecycle:** impact assessments, evaluation for accuracy and bias, red-teaming, human review where needed, logging and audit trails, incident response, monitoring after launch.
- **Governance:** a responsible AI council or risk committee with clear decision rights, involving legal, compliance, security and the business.
- **Training:** for builders and for users.

Present it as an enabler: clear rules let teams move faster with confidence.

### Client workshops and stakeholder management

#### How do you design and facilitate a use-case discovery workshop with business leaders?

**Before:**
- Agree the objective and output with the sponsor (for example a prioritised shortlist of use cases with owners).
- Invite the right mix: business leaders who own processes and budgets, a few frontline experts, IT and data, risk or compliance.
- Pre-work: short interviews and process data, so the session starts from facts; prepare examples of AI use cases relevant to their industry.

**Agenda (half day or full day):**
1. Context and goals (sponsor opens; 15 minutes).
2. A short, plain-language AI primer with relevant examples, including limits (30 minutes).
3. Pain points and opportunities by process area, in small groups (60 minutes).
4. Shape the best ideas into use-case cards: problem, users, data, value, risks (45 minutes).
5. Score value and feasibility together and plot the matrix (45 minutes).
6. Agree the shortlist, owners and next steps (30 minutes).

**Facilitation:** a neutral facilitator, timeboxes, a "parking lot" for off-topic items, inviting quieter participants, and keeping senior voices from dominating (silent idea generation first helps).

**After:** send a summary within 48 hours, then validate the business cases.

#### The client's business sponsor and their head of IT openly disagree in a workshop. What do you do?

**In the room:**
- Stay neutral and calm; acknowledge both views and restate each one accurately, so both feel heard.
- Separate facts from opinions: "What would we need to know to decide this?" Turn the disagreement into open questions or assumptions to test.
- If it cannot be resolved quickly, park it explicitly with an owner and a date, and keep the workshop moving so other participants' time is not wasted.

**After the workshop:**
- Speak to each of them privately to understand underlying concerns: the sponsor may care about speed and value; IT may care about security, support burden, architecture standards or past failures.
- Prepare a short options paper with trade-offs that addresses both sets of concerns (for example a fast pilot on an approved platform with IT's security controls).
- Bring them together, ideally with the more senior shared stakeholder if a decision is needed above them.

Never take sides publicly or let the disagreement become personal. Your value as a consultant is to make the decision easier, with evidence.

#### How do you manage scope creep on a fixed-price engagement?

- **Prevent it at the start:** a clear statement of work with deliverables, acceptance criteria, assumptions, exclusions and client responsibilities; walk the client team through it at kickoff.
- **Change control:** a simple process agreed up front: new requests are logged, assessed for effect on time and cost, and approved (or not) by the sponsor before work starts.
- **Spot it early:** creep usually arrives as small favours ("could you also add..."). Track requests, even small ones, and review them weekly.
- **Respond helpfully, not defensively:** "That is a good idea. It is outside the current scope; here are the options: swap it for something of similar size, add it as a change request with this estimate, or schedule it for the next phase."
- **Escalate early** if the client team keeps pushing, through the engagement lead and sponsor.
- **Protect relationships:** sometimes absorbing a small, high-goodwill item is the right commercial decision; make it a conscious, recorded choice rather than an accident.

In the interview, mention that fixed price shifts the overrun risk to the firm, so scope discipline directly protects margin.

#### A client stakeholder wants a recommendation that helps their department but not the company. What do you do?

1. **Understand their interest:** ask what they need and why; often there is a legitimate concern (budget, headcount, a target they are measured on) that can be addressed another way.
2. **Remember who the client is:** usually the engagement sponsor and the organisation as a whole, as defined in the contract. The recommendation must be what the evidence supports.
3. **Show the evidence:** walk them through the analysis privately, before any group presentation, so they are not surprised.
4. **Look for a solution that addresses their concern:** phasing, a role for their department in the new model, or a transition plan.
5. **If they still disagree,** do not change the conclusion; you can note their view in the presentation, and discuss it with the sponsor and your engagement partner.
6. **Keep it professional:** the stakeholder remains important to the project and future work.

The principle is integrity: a consultant's reputation depends on independent, evidence-based advice. A recommendation shaped to please one person damages the client and the firm.

### Proposals and commercial

#### How do you write a proposal or statement of work for an AI engagement?

**Proposal structure:**
1. **Understanding of the client's situation and objectives** in their language; this is where you show you listened.
2. **Proposed approach:** phases (for example discovery, pilot, scale), activities and deliverables for each, and how success will be measured.
3. **Why us:** relevant experience, assets and accelerators, the team's credentials, client references.
4. **Team and governance:** roles, onshore/offshore mix, steering committee, reporting cadence.
5. **Timeline** with milestones and decision points.
6. **Commercials:** pricing model, fees and payment milestones.
7. **Assumptions, dependencies and client responsibilities:** data access, SMEs' time, environments, security approvals.
8. **Risks and mitigations.**

**For AI specifically:** define success as measurable evaluation criteria, be clear that model quality must be validated on the client's data, include data protection and responsible AI controls, and specify ownership of code, prompts and evaluation sets.

**The statement of work** turns this into a contract: exact scope, deliverables with acceptance criteria, exclusions, change control and terms. Write it so a person who was not in the sales conversations can deliver it.

#### When would you use fixed price, time and materials, or outcome-based pricing?

- **Time and materials (T&M):** the client pays for actual effort at agreed rates. Best when scope is uncertain, such as discovery, early AI experimentation, or staff augmentation. Low risk for the firm; the client carries cost risk and needs strong oversight.
- **Fixed price:** an agreed price for a defined scope. Best when requirements are clear and the firm has delivered similar work before (for example implementing a known platform). The client gets cost certainty; the firm takes overrun risk and must manage scope tightly and include contingency.
- **Outcome-based (or gain-share):** fees linked to measured results (cost saved, claims processed, revenue increased). Attractive for clients and suitable for mature, measurable use cases with a clear baseline. Hard parts: agreeing the baseline and measurement, attribution, and the firm's dependence on factors it does not control.

**Hybrids are common:** T&M discovery, then fixed-price build, then managed service, or a lower fixed fee plus an outcome-based bonus. For AI work, uncertainty about model quality usually argues for a short T&M or fixed-fee discovery phase before committing to fixed price.

#### How do you respond to an RFP?

1. **Bid or no-bid decision first:** fit with our capabilities, relationship with the client, competition (has someone helped write the RFP?), win probability, margin and strategic value. Many firms decline bids that are poorly matched.
2. **Plan:** a bid manager, a solution lead, owners per section, and a timeline backward from the deadline with reviews.
3. **Understand evaluation criteria:** answer exactly what is asked, in the requested format; compliance failures lose bids.
4. **Clarify:** submit questions through the formal process by the deadline.
5. **Shape the solution and win themes:** three or four messages that differentiate us, backed with evidence (references, case studies, assets).
6. **Estimate and price** with a clear basis and assumptions; get commercial and legal review.
7. **Review:** an independent review of the draft from the client's point of view ("red team" review).
8. **Submit, then prepare** for orals and presentations, with the people who will actually deliver.

After the decision, request a debrief whether you win or lose and record lessons.

### Delivery

#### How do you run delivery with an onshore and offshore team across time zones?

- **Clear split of work:** onshore handles client-facing work (workshops, requirements, demos, user acceptance); offshore handles build, testing and much of the analysis, with senior technical leads in both locations.
- **Shared understanding:** a single backlog with well-written user stories and acceptance criteria, design documents and a definition of done; offshore leads attend key client sessions (at least remotely) so they hear context first-hand.
- **Overlap time:** a daily handover in the overlap window, with written updates at the end of each day so work continues around the clock without waiting for answers.
- **Rituals:** joint sprint planning, demos and retrospectives; rotate people between locations when possible to build relationships.
- **Quality:** code reviews, automated tests and CI shared by both teams; no "throw it over the wall" handoffs.
- **Communication:** document decisions, use shared tools, and prefer asynchronous written questions over waiting for meetings.
- **Team health:** respect local working hours and holidays; avoid making offshore teams carry all the late calls.

Measure velocity, defect rates and rework, and fix the process if the same issues repeat.

#### Your project is two weeks from a steering committee and behind schedule. What do you do?

1. **Get an honest picture now:** what is late, by how much, why (scope growth, dependencies, client delays, estimates, team issues), and what the critical path looks like.
2. **Recover what you can:** reprioritise to the most important deliverables, remove blockers, add or move people where it genuinely helps, and agree any client dependencies that need escalation.
3. **Tell the engagement partner and the client sponsor before the steering committee,** never in it for the first time. No surprises in front of senior people.
4. **Prepare options for the committee:** a re-baselined plan, reduced scope for the milestone, or extra resources at a cost, each with consequences and a recommendation.
5. **Present it straightforwardly:** status, root causes (including our own share), recovery plan and decisions needed. Use a clear status format (green, amber, red) and do not hide red behind amber.
6. **Follow through:** track the recovery visibly and fix the cause (for example better estimation, earlier dependency tracking).

Interviewers look for early escalation, ownership and options, not optimism or blame.

#### How do you hand over an AI solution so it keeps working after the consultants leave?

Plan handover from the start, not in the final week.

- **Ownership:** a named client product owner and operating team, involved during the build (pairing or shadowing), not introduced at the end.
- **Documentation:** architecture, data flows, prompts and configuration, model and vendor choices, evaluation set and thresholds, security controls, runbooks for common incidents, and known limitations.
- **Operations:** monitoring dashboards for quality, cost and latency; alerting; a process for reviewing samples of outputs; a change process that re-runs evaluations before any prompt, model or data change.
- **Model and vendor lifecycle:** what to do when a model version is retired or prices change; the evaluation suite makes switching safe.
- **Skills:** training for the client team, and a knowledge transfer plan with sign-off criteria (for example the client team handles a release and an incident alone).
- **Support model:** hypercare period, then a defined support arrangement (internal team, managed service or vendor).
- **Value tracking:** KPIs and an owner for reporting them.

Success is when the client team can change and run it confidently, not only when the system is live.

#### Whiteboard: design an LLM-based claims document processing solution for an insurer and explain how you would deliver it in phases.

**Clarify:** document types and volume, languages, current process and cycle time, systems (claims platform, document store), data protection constraints, and the accuracy needed.

**Design:**
1. **Ingestion:** documents arrive from email, portal and scans into secure storage; an event triggers processing.
2. **Pre-processing:** OCR for scans, document classification (claim form, invoice, medical report, police report).
3. **Extraction:** an LLM or document-AI service extracts fields into a defined schema (structured output), with validation rules (dates, amounts, policy numbers checked against the policy system).
4. **Confidence and routing:** high-confidence, low-value claims proceed automatically; low-confidence or high-value cases go to an adjuster review screen showing extracted fields alongside the source text.
5. **Integration:** results posted to the claims system via API; audit log of every decision.
6. **Evaluation and monitoring:** a labelled evaluation set, field-level accuracy metrics, sampling of production output, and drift and cost dashboards.
7. **Security:** encryption, access control, data residency and retention, approved model hosting.

**Phases:** discovery and evaluation set (4–6 weeks); pilot on one claim type with human review of all outputs (8–10 weeks); production for that type with confidence-based routing; then extend to other claim types and add fraud and triage features.

### Practice building

#### How would you help build or grow the firm's AI practice?

Contribute on four fronts, depending on level:

- **Offerings:** define a small number of repeatable offerings with clear scope and pricing (for example an AI readiness assessment, a use-case discovery sprint, a production launch package, and an AI managed service), based on what clients are buying.
- **Assets:** build accelerators that make delivery faster and more consistent: reference architectures, evaluation harnesses, document-processing templates, industry use-case libraries, and demos.
- **People:** hiring, a training curriculum (AI literacy for all consultants, deep skills for the technical track), mentoring and a community of practice to share lessons across projects.
- **Market presence:** case studies with client permission, points of view on industry problems, talks and webinars, and partnerships with cloud and AI vendors.

Tie everything to business metrics: pipeline and revenue in the practice, win rates, delivery margins, and reuse of assets. In an interview, give one concrete example of an asset or offering you would build first and why, based on what you have seen clients struggle with.

#### How do you create reusable assets and accelerators that teams actually use?

- **Harvest, do not invent:** build assets from patterns already delivered successfully on two or more projects, rather than speculating about what teams might need.
- **Solve a real, frequent pain:** for example a standard evaluation harness, a secure RAG reference implementation, or proposal templates for common offerings.
- **Make them easy to adopt:** good documentation, a quick start, examples, and a demo; designed to be configured, not forked.
- **Ownership and maintenance:** a named owner, a version history, and a review schedule; AI tooling ages quickly, and a stale asset is worse than none.
- **Distribution:** a searchable catalogue, internal showcases, and inclusion in project start-up checklists and training.
- **Feedback loop:** collect issues and improvements from project teams; credit contributors in performance reviews.
- **Measure use:** how many projects used it, time saved, and win-rate effect, and retire assets that nobody uses.

Mention client IP: make sure assets do not contain client confidential information and that contracts allow reuse of generic components.

### Behavioral (consulting fit)

#### Tell me about a time you influenced a senior person without having authority over them.

**What they look for:** at strategy firms this is often the "personal impact" theme, explored in depth for 10–15 minutes. They want to hear your specific actions, how you read the other person, how you adapted when the first approach did not work, and the result. Expect follow-ups such as "What exactly did you say?" and "How did they react?"

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The senior person, their position, and why their agreement mattered]
- **T:** [What you needed them to do or change]
- **A:** [How you understood their concerns, the evidence you prepared, the setting you chose, how you adapted after initial resistance, who else you brought in]
- **R:** [What they decided, the result for the organisation, the relationship afterwards]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "A finance head opposed changing the discount approval process because they feared losing control over revenue leakage. I met them one to one, asked what reports they relied on, and found their concern was visibility, not the process. I proposed rules-based approvals with a weekly exception report designed with their team. They agreed to a one-month trial, which approved routine discounts faster while giving them better visibility, and they later sponsored rolling it out to other teams."

#### Tell me about a time you led a team through a difficult situation.

**What they look for:** leadership under pressure: how you set direction, kept people motivated, made trade-offs, dealt with conflict or underperformance, and took responsibility. Consulting firms look for people who lead with or without a title.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The team, the difficulty (tight deadline, crisis, conflict, a key person leaving)]
- **T:** [Your role and what had to be achieved]
- **A:** [How you clarified priorities, reorganised work, supported people, communicated with stakeholders, and handled the hardest moment]
- **R:** [Outcome with numbers, team feedback, what you learned about leading]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "Two weeks before a major release, a critical data migration showed mismatches, and the team was tired and blaming each other. I stopped new feature work, split the team into pairs on the highest-risk areas, set up a daily 15-minute reconciliation review, and handled stakeholder updates myself so the team could focus. We fixed the root causes, released one week later than planned with all reconciliations passing, and the retrospective led to automated reconciliation checks we used on later migrations."

#### Tell me about a time you had to learn a new domain quickly to deliver for a client or stakeholder.

**What they look for:** consultants change industries and topics constantly. They want a deliberate learning approach (sources, experts, first-principles questions), speed, humility, and evidence that the learning changed the work.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The new domain and why you needed it]
- **T:** [What you had to deliver and by when]
- **A:** [How you learned: key documents and data, experts you interviewed, how you checked your understanding, how you applied it]
- **R:** [What you delivered, how domain experts judged it, how quickly you became useful]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "I was asked to contribute to an evaluation of speech-to-text options without prior experience in speech technology. In the first week I read provider documentation and accuracy reports, spoke with colleagues who used the transcripts, and built a simple comparison of costs and data-protection requirements. Within a few weeks I was contributing to the buy-versus-build cost model, and the evaluation gave the decision-makers a clear comparison of the options."

#### Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news to a client or senior stakeholder.

**What they look for:** honesty, timing (early, not at the last moment), preparation, ownership, and options rather than just problems. They also want to see that the relationship survived.

**STAR outline:**
- **S:** [The context and the bad news (delay, cost overrun, a disappointing result, a wrong assumption)]
- **T:** [Why you were the right person to deliver it]
- **A:** [How you verified the facts, prepared options, chose the setting, delivered the message clearly, and handled their reaction]
- **R:** [Their decision, the project outcome, and the effect on trust]

**Example (adapt to your own experience):** "Midway through a project, testing showed that a planned automation would only handle about half the cases we had assumed, so the business case was weaker than presented. I verified the results with the team, then met the sponsor before the steering committee. I explained what we found and why our assumption had been wrong, and offered two options: narrow the scope to the cases it handled well, or invest in better data first. They chose the narrower scope, the launch went well, and the sponsor told me they valued hearing it early."

## Prep checklist

1. Learn case interview fundamentals: issue trees, hypothesis-driven work, case maths and synthesis; practise at least 15–20 live cases with partners, mixing candidate-led and interviewer-led formats, including at least five AI or technology cases.
2. Do daily mental maths and estimation drills (percentages, growth rates, break-even, market sizing) until you can calculate out loud without errors.
3. Prepare four to six deep stories (influence, leadership, overcoming a challenge, learning fast, delivering bad news, a failure) and practise answering 10 minutes of follow-up questions on each; use [10 Behavioral](../../topics/10-behavioral/ANSWERS.md).
4. Prepare a one-page AI strategy framework, a use-case prioritisation matrix and a readiness assessment you can draw from memory.
5. Refresh AI depth so you can explain RAG, agents, fine-tuning, evaluation, cost and risk in plain language: [06 AI / LLM](../../topics/06-ai-llm/ANSWERS.md) and [part 2](../../topics/06-ai-llm/ANSWERS-part2.md).
6. Practise one solution architecture whiteboard (document processing or a knowledge assistant) with a phased delivery plan, using [03 System design](../../topics/03-system-design/ANSWERS.md).
7. Write a sample two-page proposal and a five-slide executive summary for an AI engagement; check that each slide title states the conclusion.
8. Research each firm: its AI practice, recent published points of view, industries, level structure, and the interview process described on its careers site.
9. If you have done delivery management, prepare numbers: team sizes, budgets, timelines, onshore/offshore structure, and outcomes, all accurate.
10. Prepare questions about the practice, staffing model, travel, utilisation expectations and how technical consultants progress, using [14 Questions to ask](../../topics/14-questions-to-ask/ANSWERS.md).
